## 1. SkySails ship kites (2007–2016)

### How launch and recovery worked

- System: ram-air towing kite, a control pod hanging below it on the line tree, one towing rope (~300 m operational length for the 160 m² kite), and a launch and recovery system on the forecastle (Erhard 2013, Fig. 1 and Fig. 18; SkySails 2018 slide "Towing kite & control pod": towing kite, kite adapter, line tree, control pod, towing rope, control belt). The pod steers by driving a toothed belt that shortens one steering line against the other, at 0.3–0.5 1/s (Erhard 2013, Fig. 2, Table I).
- Mechanism (SkySails patent US 7,866,271, "Placement system for a flying kite-type wind-attacked element", Wrage and Böhm, https://patents.google.com/patent/US7866271): a telescopic, hydraulically driven crane whose top carries a docking receptacle that rotates in azimuth; the kite is held at one point of its leading edge; an inflatable bead in the leading edge stiffens the wing and helps unreef it; reefing strips driven by a winch in the wing gather the canopy; a fan inflates and deflates the wing through a leading-edge opening; on recovery a lifeline through the crane pulls the wing onto the mast, holding means lock it automatically, the crane is lowered and the wing is folded and stowed. Wikipedia's _Kite rig_ summarises this as "an animated mast or arm, which grips the kite by its leading edge … The mast also inflates and deflates the kite. When not in use, mast and deflated kite fold away" (citing Cherubini et al., _RSER_ 2015).
- Duration: "The fully automatic launch and landing took about 10–20 minutes each" (de.wikipedia _Beluga SkySails_, citing Höltkemeier, _Konstruktionspraxis_, 2008); Soundings Online likewise: "automatic and takes 10 to 20 minutes", with "a winch and telescopic mast that lifts the kite into and out of a storage container" (https://soundingsonline.com/news/fuel-costs-spark-talk-of-kite-power/, undated, ~2008).
- Wind window: designed for Beaufort 3–8; "The system can be recovered, but not launched at wind forces below 3 Beaufort"; kite flies 100–300 m above the ship; crew training is a two-day course, and the system "requires the occasional help and watchful eye of a trained crew" (J. Konrad, _Skysails_, NOAA VOS _Mariners Weather Log_ 53(1), Apr 2009, https://vos.noaa.gov/MWL/apr_09/skysails.shtml).

### How reliable it was in service

- Fleet experience in total: "more than 1200 automated kite flights", "non-stop air time of up to 120 hours with a full-scale kite on a customer vessel (until the wind calmed down)", "more than 1,800 hours of air time" (SkySails 2018, slide "SkySails in numbers"). That is 1.5 h per flight on average, and 1,800 h over roughly five ship installations in 2007–2013: a small fraction of the hours at sea. Launch and landing were automated and repeatable; utilisation was low.
- Beluga SkySails maiden voyage (Bremen–Guanta, Jan–Mar 2008, 160 m²): kite flown "on several days between a few minutes and eight hours" over 51 days (SkySails press release 14 Mar 2008, via de.wikipedia); "on average the kite could be flown three to four hours a day", saving on average half a tonne of HFO a day, and "the Atlantic swell posed a particular problem for the crew during kite operation" (VerkehrsRundschau, 20 Feb 2008, https://www.verkehrsrundschau.de/nachrichten/transport-logistik/sky-sail-einsatz-erfolgreich-im-praxiseinsatz-3025027). On arrival in Rotterdam "storms prevented the vessel from deploying its SkySails system" (Lloyd's List, 6 Feb 2009, https://www.lloydslist.com/LL051954/Beluga-sans-SkySails).
- Wessels (Michael A., 2007; Theseus from Aug 2009), as quoted by CE Delft 2016 §4.5.2: "the operating time of the kites was suboptimal due to limited launch opportunities during heavy sea and some of the component's resilience and stamina when exposed to harsh conditions at sea were suboptimal too. As a consequence, the company developed an integrated launch and recovery module with … improved endurance of the components and improved launching features, which also made operating easier for the crew." SkySails' CEO in 2011: "Full-scale tests showed us that some components and equipment did not quite measure up" (Teknisk Ukeblad, 15 Jun 2011, https://www.tu.no/artikler/industri-skysails-fikk-fart-i-seilene/247909).
- Operators, also CE Delft 2016 §4.5.2: a charterer that bought a kite ship uninstalled the system, and a long-term charterer stopped using an installed one; reasons given were that it "would need specific wind conditions and could only be operated at daylight", was not cost efficient on short voyages, would have needed routing to windy routes, and "the system was assessed to be too labour intensive and crew training was stated to be a challenge, given that the charterer's crews change on a regular basis." In 2016 "the system is still installed on three vessels but currently not actively used."
- Founder's own verdict in 2018 on Beluga SkySails: "That was one of our first prototypes. In hindsight, the handling system for launching and landing would have to be optimised" (Stephan Wrage in Weser-Kurier, 14 Sep 2018, https://www.weser-kurier.de/bremen/wirtschaft/airbus-bringt-zugdrachen-fuer-frachtschiffe-auf-den-markt-doc7e47ufm91yb83381nsa). Under its next owner the ship sailed as BBC SkySails without using the kite (same article).
- Trawler Maartje Theadora (2010): the system was removed in mid-2012 "because the towing kite system proved unsuitable for trawl fishing" (DVZ, _Flaute statt Höhenflug_, 18 Mar 2013, cited by de.wikipedia; the DVZ original was not reachable).
- Cargill/Aghia Marina (320 m² kite, contracted 2011 for Q1 2012): an In These Times article (2019) reports Cargill citing "encountered obstacles" by 2015, the ship sold in 2017 and the kite removed around then. Not verified: the article returned 403; only the search snippet was read.
- Fuel savings: 5 % on an average route mix and 10–12 % on North Atlantic and North Pacific routes on Beluga SkySails in the EU LIFE WINTECC project (LIFE06 ENV/D/000479, 2006–2009, https://webgate.ec.europa.eu/life/publicWebsite/project/LIFE06-ENV-D-000479/demonstration-of-an-innovative-wind-propulsion-technology-for-cargo-vessels), far below the 10–35 % SkySails advertised (t-online, 11 Feb 2012). SkySails' later slides claim 35–40 % on Theseus and 30 % on BBC SkySails for single voyages (SkySails 2018); EMSA 2023 quotes Theseus at 40 % at 10 kn and 17 % at 4 kn. Peak pull "up to 17 tonnes" on Beluga SkySails (WINTECC, no kite size given); "up to 8 tons of tractive force" on Michael A. (Gerd Wessels via Surfertoday, read through search snippet only).

### Why the marine business stopped

- Early 2012: 40 of 80 staff laid off; "around ten towing kites sold, but not all of them installed yet"; shipowners would not invest "around one million euros in a towing kite" given their uncertain situation and no bank support (t-online/dpa, 11 Feb 2012, https://www.t-online.de/finanzen/aktuelles/wirtschaft/id_53937564/skysails-zugdrachen-ziehen-nicht-richtig.html). "Four are currently in operation worldwide on freighters and trawlers"; the last kite was sold almost a year earlier (NWZ, 2012, https://www.nwzonline.de/wirtschaft/gegenwind-fuer-schiffsdrachen_a_1,0,530192929.html).
- 2015: only two ships using the system; Wrage blames the post-2008 shipping slump (CNBC, 14 Sep 2015, https://www.cnbc.com/2015/09/14/how-kites-can-save-the-planet--or-the-shipping-industry.html).
- SkySails GmbH filed for insolvency on 7 Mar 2016 and the wind-propulsion part was liquidated in April 2016 (CE Delft 2016 §4.5.2, citing Handelsregister). SkySails Group kept a yacht system (2016–2018) and moved to power generation; SkySails Power GmbH itself filed for insolvency on 9 Dec 2025 (Energie & Management, 12 Dec 2025, cited by de.wikipedia).
- Sales stopped in 2011–2012, but the marine business formally ended with the 2016 insolvency. The reasons given are economic (shipping crisis, falling oil price, payback many years, lower-than-promised savings) plus the operational limits above. No source blames a single technical failure.

## 2. Airseas Seawing (2016–, K Line since 2024)

- System: a ram-air parafoil of 1,000 m² for the commercial unit ("1000m² of sails", ~100 t traction claimed); a deck platform with winches and a 35 m mast; "a deck cart … releases the wing from its storage before being inflated at the top of the mast"; the wing "unfolds at the top of a 35m mast"; the flight-control pod has "an autopilot and 3 actuators" and is linked to the ship by an umbilical cable carrying traction, data and power; deployment takes "30mn" at the push of a button, with "no crew intervention required"; installation "24 hours", bolted on, no dry dock (Airseas product page, https://airseas.com/en/seawing-system/, read 2026-10-31; all company claims). The umbilical means the pod needs no battery for endurance.
- Trials: a 500 m² Seawing on the 5,291 dwt ro-ro Ville de Bordeaux (Louis Dreyfus Armateurs), fitted 2021, sea trials from Jan 2022 (Wikipedia, _Ville de Bordeaux_). Airseas reported validated "automated take-off and landing phases", static traction flights, then automated figure-eight flights "without any human intervention", projecting 16 % savings on a normalised transatlantic voyage from measured traction (Airseas 2023). First "tonnes of traction" and "launches or recovers the kite which unfolds, operates and refolds autonomously" (Riviera, 19 May 2023, https://www.rivieramm.com/news-content-hub/news-content-hub/wind-power-pioneer-airseas-demonstrates-traction-flight-as-seawing-aims-for-large-scale-production-76211).
- K Line: 5 firm units with options for up to 51 (Airseas 2023); first installation on a 210k dwt Capesize reported Jan 2023 (autoevolution, 6 Jan 2023); K Line created Oceanicwing and bought the Airseas business on 15 Feb 2024, price undisclosed; the 2024 commercial-production target was missed and Louis Dreyfus/Airbus chose Norsepower rotors for their three new ro-ros (Maritime Executive, 16 Feb 2024, https://maritime-executive.com/article/japan-s-k-line-buys-airseas-developer-of-kite-wind-propulsion-system). Phase one finished Jun 2025 with a 300 m² kite on a land site (Dakhla); phase two enlarges the kite, about two years of tests; savings now stated as "more than 10%" (Airseas/K Line, 22 Sep 2025, https://airseas.com/en/phase-one-development-seawing-automated-kite-system-harnessing-natural-wind-power-completed/).
- Reliability: no flight hours, launch counts or failure rates have been published. One unsourced article claims a "95% deployment success rate" in phase one and "installation costs €2-5 million per unit" (FesNews, 17 Sep 2025, https://fesnews.media/311533/2025/09/17/). Weak source; not used.
- Price: not published ("request a personalized quotation", Airseas site).

## 3. LEI or ram-air for launch and landing at sea

### Arguments from the literature and practice

- Every ship kite flown at full scale, SkySails at 160–400 m² and Airseas at 500–1,000 m², is a ram-air wing launched from a mast that holds the leading edge. Both needed extra means to give the ram-air wing shape at zero airspeed: SkySails an inflatable leading-edge bead, a fan and reefing strips (US 7,866,271); Airseas inflation at the mast top (Airseas site). The trouble SkySails had was with the handling system, not the canopy type (Wessels via CE Delft 2016; Wrage 2018).
- LEI arguments: the tube holds the shape without airflow, so the kite can be held, folded or rolled on a tower and relaunched; Kitepower switched from ram-air because "ram-air wings can't be depowered much without them completely folding up", but warned that LEI wings "may prove too flexible to execute a reliable retract cycle" and are hard to reinforce for hard landings (R. Creighton, _IEEE Spectrum_, 28 Nov 2012, https://spectrum.ieee.org/the-benefits-of-airborne-wind-energy). In kitesurfing an LEI floats and relaunches from water; an open-cell foil fills with water and can sink, a closed-cell foil can relaunch (kitesurf guides, e.g. https://sites.google.com/a/peterskiteboarding.com/kitesurfing-handbook/gear/foil-kites).
- LEI limits: the largest LEI kites in operation are Kitepower's 60–80 m² V9 (Falcon datasheet) and the 100 m² design of Faggiani 2018. Tube buckling load scales as pressure × diameter³ / length (kite designer blog, https://switchkitesriders.blogspot.com/2014/05/kite-tube-size-is-bigger-better-by-bill.html; not peer reviewed), so an LEI cannot be scaled by area alone. No LEI near 300–1,000 m² has flown. Kitepower, an LEI developer, still needed "a group of people … to deploy and retrieve these kites" in 2023 (TU Delft DSE 2023); its automatic launch, storage and relaunch is a 2030 target.
- Aerodynamics: in a steady aeroelastic simulation of Kitepower's kites the LEI wing outperformed the ram-air wing because of its higher aspect ratio (Folkersma, Schmehl & Viré, _Steady-state aeroelasticity of a ram-air wing for airborne wind energy applications_, J. Phys. Conf. Ser. 1618, 032018, 2020, https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1742-6596/1618/3/032018/pdf; read through search snippet).

### Canopy lifetime

"Flight hours" means hours flown or set under load. A ship kite flies figure-eights at a fixed tether length (steady towing load, no reeling under load); an AWE pumping kite powers and depowers every 60–100 s cycle. A sail is loaded steadily, never flies crosswind at 3–5× wind speed and flexes and luffs less than a kite, though per unit width of cloth a sail and a ship kite carry about the same tension (`canopy_life_notes.md`); both suffer UV, but a kite is stowed out of the sun when not flying while a sail stays rigged (covered or furled) between uses. Sail life read in hours is therefore a ceiling on UV-limited life and an upper guide for flex life. Conversions: miles at the stated average speed; days × 24 h for races where the mainsail is set throughout.

The only rating from a kite maker is Kitepower's 4,000 h for a 60–80 m² pumping LEI kite, and the flight hours Kitepower has published (>100 h in the first year at the Irish site, ~95 h in 79 days of 2025) are far short of demonstrating it. Sails under steady load reach 1,700–4,000 h, and an IMOCA mainsail at least 1,550–2,800 h. A ship kite works at 0.4–0.5 kN/m², flies crosswind and luffs at every launch and landing, which argues for less than a sail's life; it is stowed out of the sun, which argues that UV alone will not end it before ~2,000 flight hours. Small sport wings (200–800 h) are lighter-built and hand-handled and set the low end. Nothing published supports more than 4,000 h for any kite; `canopy_life_notes.md` derives the life from the cloth.

A ship tether is not cycled over the drum under load, so the pumping mechanism that sets the Joshi and DEADALUS lives is absent, and creep at the mean towing load (DSM) allows far more than 4,000 h. But the tether leaves the bow over a fairlead or sheave and swings with every figure-eight, bending the same short section under full load ~100 times an hour (estimate: one loop every 30–40 s), a count comparable to the Joshi drum cycles; abrasion, UV and handling at launch and landing add to it. No fixed-length towing tether life is published. `tether_lifetime` stays at 4,000 h (2,000–16,000): the low end is the shortest pumping life, the high end the SK78 creep life in warm exposed conditions.

Kitepower, SkySails Power (Mauritius, PN-14) and K Line/Oceanicwing have published no measured canopy replacement intervals or fleet flight hours; ask them directly.

### Choice of canopy type

SkySails and Airseas both chose ram-air because it is the type that scales to several hundred m²; an LEI is easier to hold, depower and recover from the water but is unproven above ~100 m². For a ship kite of 300–1,000 m², ram-air (with an inflated leading-edge stiffener, as SkySails patented) is the defensible choice; LEI fits kites of ≤100–150 m². Launch and landing reliability depends on the handling system more than on canopy type, and no source quantifies it for either.

## 4. Component series prices

### a. Holding winch

- What sets the size: brake holding load, not line pull. Mooring winch brakes are designed to hold 80 % of the line's MBL and set in service at 60 % (Wärtsilä encyclopedia, https://wartsila.com/encyclopedia/term/mooring-winch-brake-design-capacity-brake-holding-capacity). Pull/hold pairs on the market: 100 t pull with 200 t brake (Unique Group); 68 t pull, 79 t hold, 38 mm × 1,200 m drum (Taixing Expansion, directindustry.com). A kite winch needs low pull and a large brake, which matches a mooring winch. A brake set to render (slip) at a load limit also protects the tether in gusts.

### b. Launch mast

- Heights in use: Airseas 35 m for 1,000 m² (Airseas site); SkySails a telescopic crane, height not published. Both ends fit a mast height of ~0.7–1.1 × √(kite area).
- Price signals: 20–35 m hot-dip galvanised high-mast lighting poles USD 2,890 (pole only) to 8,300–8,500 (pole, base plate, cross arm, anchors) FOB China (madeinchina.com listing 998345; china.org.ru listing 60726970490), i.e. 100–250 USD/m; an aluminium yacht replacement mast ~USD 30,000 in 2009, boat size unstated (Morgan's Cloud, read through search snippet); a full new Seldén rig USD 30–40 k for an unstated boat (Cruisers Forum); mast extrusion 12.5–34 USD/ft in 2015 (Cape Cod Shipbuilding). No yacht-mast price list (Seldén, Sparcraft, Southern Spars, Z-Spars) is published; all quote on request. No telescopic mast price for 30 m heavy duty was found (Will-Burt and others quote on request).
- Faggiani 2018 launching and landing: C = c·M_kite·√A = EUR 4,800 for a 48 kg, 100 m² kite on land (Table 16.2). Extrapolated by its own formula to a ~500 kg, 1,000 m² ship kite it gives ~EUR 160 k (estimate: kite mass assumed).
- Proposed (estimate): a marinised steel mast costs ~4,000 USD per m of height, plus USD 60 k for the slewing docking head, folding/storage and actuators. Reasoning: a 35 m tube of 0.8 m diameter and 12 mm wall weighs ~240 kg/m; at an assumed 5–10 USD/kg fabricated, coated and installed that is 1,200–2,400 USD/m, and the rest covers foundation, rigging and the telescopic or folding mechanism. At h = √A this is USD 117 k for 200 m² and USD 186 k for 1,000 m², close to the Faggiani extrapolation.

### c. Kite control unit

- Faggiani 2018: EUR 3,000 for a 100 m² kite in series production, formula c₁ + c₂√A (Table 16.2); KCU mass 16 kg (Table 16.1).
- SkySails pod: steering by toothed belt at 0.3–0.5 1/s, IMU and anemometer on board (Erhard 2013). Airseas: autopilot and 3 actuators, power through the umbilical (Airseas site). Kitepower: an early KCU's battery limited flights to ~5 h; the newer one no longer depends on battery power and carries a small wind turbine (TU Delft Delta, https://delta.tudelft.nl/?p=71017 and ?p=71019; read through search snippets). The figure-eight traction kite of this model is in that class: endurance comes from an umbilical or an on-board turbine, not a large battery.
- Part prices: Teknic ClearPath integrated servo with drive, 0.9–5.8 kW peak / up to 3.0 kW RMS, from USD 698 (teknic.com, read through search snippet); Li-ion packs USD 108/kWh average, 182/kWh outside China (BNEF survey, 9 Dec 2025, https://about.bnef.com/insights/clean-energy/lithium-ion-battery-pack-prices-fall-to-108-per-kilowatt-hour-despite-rising-metal-prices-bloombergnef/).
- Proposed (estimate): USD 10 k + 20 USD/m² × kite area, so USD 16 k at 300 m² and USD 30 k at 1,000 m². Bottom-up: three actuators at 2–5 kUSD each (servo, drive, gearbox and drum, scaled with steering force), 2–5 kUSD for flight computer, IMU, GNSS and radio, 3–10 kUSD for a sealed marine housing and line attachment, under 1 kUSD for a 2 kWh buffer battery, times 1.5 for assembly, test and margin. A ship pod needs ~5–10× the steering force of Faggiani's 100 m² unit, which is why it costs several times their EUR 3 k.

### e. Integration and installation

- EMSA 2023 (Table 7 assumptions): installation on a newbuild 15 % of the asset cost, retrofit another 10 %; training EUR 10 k; maintenance 5 % of CAPEX a year for kites against 2 % for other WAPS, because "wear and tear can be expected to [be] relatively high for kites". Kite rows: newbuild 51–351 kEUR, retrofit 85–586 kEUR.
- Airseas claims a 24 h bolted installation without dry dock (Airseas site). SkySails systems were commissioned in its Wismar facility (Surfertoday, via search snippet).
- The deck foundation must carry the full tether load; EMSA 2023 judges the reinforcement "not an insurmountable technical or financial barrier" but gives no figure.

## 5. Published techno-economic studies

- **EMSA 2023**: the only public per-unit kite CAPEX/OPEX table (Table 7, quoted in §1 and §4e). Built from IMO GHG4 and Schenker (Bloomberg, Nov 2007) plus interviews with rotor and suction-wing suppliers, none with a kite supplier; treat its kite row as weak.
- **CE Delft 2016**: payback model from supplier questionnaires, with a 400 m² kite; it does not publish the kite's CAPEX. Its §4.5.2 SkySails case study is the best source on why the system failed commercially. Kite power savings 1–9 % at full speed and 2–15 % at slow speed.
- **Faggiani 2018**: full component cost model for a series-built 100 m² LEI pumping system (Table 16.2: kite EUR 22 k, KCU EUR 3 k, tether EUR 9 k, drum EUR 3.2 k, tether handling EUR 9 k, launching and landing EUR 4.8 k, unit total EUR 98.8 k; consumables EUR 17 k a year). The closest public breakdown for the airborne parts.
- **BVGA 2022**: no component costs; only LCOE trajectories (99 EUR/MWh in 2025 to 15 in 2050) and cost shares (CAPEX 15 % of discounted project cost for AWE against ~40 % for established wind, because AWE replaces components as OPEX).
- **IMO GreenVoyage2050**: USD 0.25–4.2 M for 160–5,000 m², maintenance 2 % a year, kite usable 20–30 % of the time.
- Traut et al. (_Applied Energy_ 113, 2014), Dadd et al. (2010), Naaijen et al. (2006), Leloup (2016): performance studies; none found with hardware costs. Their full texts were not checked for cost tables.
