\section{How far the method converges}
\label{app:convergence}

The error that the verdicts between rigs carry from the method itself: the router's grid and search, the forecast, the scatter of routes of nearly equal cost, and the size sweep behind the missed-goal figures (\cref{sec:res_robustness}).

\subsection{Convergence of the router}
\label{app:routing_convergence}

\stale{router_grid}{The router's grid and search are judged on the 12 departures of the \SI{140}{m} e-ammonia ship to Guadeloupe without a rig, with \SI{3200}{m^2} of kite and with \SI{800}{m^2} of rotors, departure by departure; the wing sails move least with any setting and are left out. A setting is accepted when each rig's cost saving moves by at most 3 points from a reference and the gap between the two rigs by at most 1 point, and of the settings accepted the cheapest to compute is taken. The reference would be the planner at a setting where twice as fine moves each saving by less than 0.25 points, but no grid we can afford gets there. Refining the grid and every time step together, from stages 240 to 120, 60 and \SI{30}{nmi} apart with rows a quarter of that and time bins from 12 to \SI{1.5}{h}, raises the kite's saving by 4.0, 2.2 and 1.3 points and the rotors' by 1.8, 1.1 and 0.6 (\cref{tab:routing_grid}). These leave out the one departure where the finest grid finds a trade-wind route that the coarser ones miss, which raises the kite's saving there by 7.8 points; the grid of the results finds it too. Each step is about half the one before: the order fitted to the three coarsest grids predicts the finest within 0.15 points for each rig. The finest grid is the reference. Extrapolated from the three finest grids, at the fitted order or at order one, the converged savings lie 1.3 to 1.8 points above the reference's for the kite and 0.6 to 0.7 for the rotors, so each error measured against the reference understates the true one by about that much. Refining the grid alone is not enough: on stages \SI{30}{nmi} apart a stage takes \SI{2.5}{h} to sail, less than a \SI{3}{h} time bin, and halving every time step there raises the kite's saving by 2.7 points and the rotors' by 0.3.}

\stale{router_grid}{The grid of the results, stages \SI{45}{nmi} and rows \SI{11.25}{nmi} apart with the plan coarsening beyond the first \SI{240}{nmi}, meets the standard against the reference. On stages \SI{60}{nmi} and rows \SI{15}{nmi} apart the kite's gap to the rotors moves by 1.2 points, which does not. There the grid carries the error rather than the time steps: halving the time bins alone moves the kite by 0.3 points and the rotors by 0.1. Keeping every fourth row to the end of each plan, rather than half as many each time the distance ahead doubles, moves the kite by 0.5 points and the rotors by less than 0.1; keeping every stage and row for the first \SI{480}{nmi}, or every second row at first, moves them less. Every eighth row from the first \SI{240}{nmi} on loses the kite a further 1.6 points on stages \SI{60}{nmi} apart. The search of the whole corridor at once, on stages \SI{240}{nmi} apart, understates the kite by 5.3 points.}

\stale{router_grid}{A draft run routes on the grid of the results but on one in six of the final run's departures, since a coarser grid would bias it. A draft on stages \SI{240}{nmi} apart and half the departures would understate the kite's saving by 4 points and its gap to the rotors by 2.2. On one in six of the departures a draft saving has no bias from the grid and lies about the final run's with a spread of \NumDraftSpreadKite{} points for the kite, \NumDraftSpreadRotors{} for the rotors and \NumDraftSpreadGap{} for the gap between them, measured on this case.}

\stale{router_grid}{Once chosen, the grid was checked once on every rig, for the \SI{140}{m} ship to Guadeloupe, New York and Houston and the Kamsarmax to Tubarão, 12 departures each, against stages \SI{30}{nmi} and rows \SI{7.5}{nmi} apart at the time steps of the results, a grid that every route can afford. Every cost saving lies within 1.7 points of it and every gap between two rigs within 0.7 points, except the kite's gaps to the rotors and to the wing sails to Guadeloupe, 2.0 and 1.6 points, where that grid's coarse time steps understate the kite by the 2.7 points above. Against the plan that keeps every fourth row to the end, every saving lies within 1.4 points and every gap within 1 point. Every case found a passage on both grids. Bilbao, whose short crossing only the floor on open-sea stages gives a grid, is checked apart, so that one short route cannot set every route's grid: its savings move by at most 0.7 points and its gaps by at most 0.8, though one departure with rotors loses 9.5 points, sailing slower from the start because the coarse far part of its early plans across Biscay values a later arrival.}

\subsection{Forecast plans}
\label{app:forecast_studies}

\studyresult{forecast_causes,forecast_live,forecast_replanning}{Sailed at the plan's speed over each edge of about a day, the kite loses $3.1 \pm 0.7$ points more of its cost saving on the forecasts than the rotors, over 22 departures. The excess comes from the wind forecast, not the waves, and from that held speed. A deck rig's pull falls a little when the wind is a little off; the kite's stops at its lowest flying wind and outside its launch range, so a speed held for a flying kite is lost when it cannot fly, and a slow one held for a kite down is kept when it could. A master sets the speed hour by hour on the wind met, and a ship sailed so loses the kite $0.1 \pm 0.9$ points more than the rotors, or $0.7 \pm 0.7$ against perfect foresight sailed the same way, no excess clear of the error, so the forecast result is sailed that way. On this ship and route the order of the rigs is the same with perfect foresight and on the forecasts. Re-planning more often than once a day changes what the kite and the rotors keep within the error and may understate what the wing sails keep by up to 10 points.}

\subsection{Routing noise}
\label{sec:routing_noise}

A change of \given{2}\,\% in the tether length or the smallest turn radius should barely move a kite's cost saving. \stale{base_kite}{On three ships with kites of \SI{3200}{m^2}, \SI{1448}{m^2} and \SI{16000}{m^2}, the base and those four changes span 1.4, 0.8 and 1.5 points of cost saving and 6.6, 4.9 and 2.3 points of fuel saving, in the method study \studyrun{base_kite}.} The router minimises cost, so routes of nearly equal cost can burn different amounts of fuel. We do not read a smaller difference between two kites as a result.

\subsection{Error of the missed-goal figures}
\label{sec:check_missed}

\stale{goal_size_data,canopy_price_data,canopy_life_data}{All limits come from one size sweep over the same \given{6} departures, around the size the class study gives each rig: half to twice for wing sails and rotors as far as they fit on deck, and a quarter to four times for the kite up to the search bound of \given{\SI{16000}{m^2}}. The best kite lies inside the sizes swept on all three ships. The best wing sails are the largest that fit on deck on the \SI{140}{m} ship and the Neo-Panamax and the best rotors on the Neo-Panamax. The rest lie inside the sizes swept.}

\stale{goal_size_data,canopy_price_data,canopy_life_data}{Routing again at each canopy life moves the limits little: on the Neo-Panamax on e-ammonia to New York the limit with the route held is \num{1610} flight hours and the one routed afresh at each life \num{1600}.}

\stale{goal_size_data,canopy_price_data}{\Cref{sec:res_missed} gives what a more expensive canopy takes from the kite on three ships. On three departures of a draft, the routes changing between the base price and one about half again higher moved these figures by 0.1 points at most. A line through the prices from USD~112 to 375/m$^2$ gave 1.6, 1.6 and 0.7 points against 1.7, 1.6 and 0.8 between the two prices. Leaving out of maintenance the canopy and tether that wear already pays for was worth 0.3, 0.15 and 0.24 points of cost saving on the three ships.}
